What does car refinance mean in practice?

Car refinance replaces the agreement on a car with a new one. The new lender pays off the old agreement using the settlement figure, takes security over the car and collects the payments from then on. The car stays where it is, and so do you.

People come to it for one of three reasons. Some want a smaller monthly payment. Some face a large final payment at the end of a personal contract purchase or lease purchase agreement and want to keep the car. Some own a car worth well above what they owe and want to draw cash out as part of the switch, which is car equity release by another name.

Much of what ranks for this search is mainstream switching: moving a modest balance on an everyday car to a cheaper lender. That is a useful service, and for an ordinary car with a small balance, a comparison site or a call to your current lender may be all you need.

We do something narrower. We arrange car refinance where the car needs valuing as an individual car rather than being read off a trade price guide, where money is released at the same time, or where the borrowing is for a business. We do not publish a minimum advance, and the model values we publish run from £30,000 to £5,400,000.

Is car refinance worth it? Compare totals, not payments

There is one test. Add up everything the new agreement will cost across its whole term, fees included, and set it against everything staying put would cost, including any early settlement charge on the old agreement. If the new total is lower, the refinance saves money. If it is higher, the refinance may still be the right decision, but it is a trade of total cost for cash flow, and it should be made knowingly.

Here is the opening example again, a hypothetical £60,000 settlement figure with 18 months left at 11.9 per cent:

OptionMonthly paymentMonthsTotal payable
Stay on the current agreement at 11.9 per cent£3,65618£65,810
Refinance at 8.9 per cent over 36 months£1,90536£68,587
Refinance at 8.9 per cent over 48 months£1,49048£71,532

The rate falls by three percentage points and the payment falls by as much as £2,166 a month, yet both refinance options cost more overall, because interest now runs for two or nearly three times as long. Fees and any early settlement charge would widen the gap further.

Refinance does save money when the rate falls and the term stays roughly where it was, or when the old agreement is expensive enough that the saving outruns the extra months. The only way to know is to put both totals side by side, in writing.

A refinance saves money when the total payable falls, and only then. A lower monthly figure bought with a longer term is a trade, not a saving.

For a business, paying more over four years to free up £2,166 a month this quarter can be a perfectly sound call. The mistake is not the trade. It is failing to notice the trade was made.

Settling the old agreement and releasing capital in one go

When a car is worth well above its settlement figure, a refinance can do two jobs at once: clear the old agreement and release part of the value. Take a hypothetical car valued at £180,000 with the same £60,000 to settle.

  • A lender advancing 70 per cent of value lends £180,000 times 0.70, which is £126,000.
  • £60,000 of that goes straight to the outgoing lender.
  • £66,000 is paid to you or into your business.

The payment runs on the full £126,000. At 8.9 per cent the monthly rate is 0.089 divided by 12, or 0.0074167, and over 48 months (1 + 0.0074167) raised to the power of 48 is 1.4257. The payment is £126,000 times 0.0074167, which is £934.50, divided by 1 minus 1 over 1.4257, which is 0.2986. That comes to about £3,130 a month and £150,218 across the term.

So this owner pays a little less each month than on the old agreement and has £66,000 in hand, but over 48 months rather than 18, on a far larger sum. Asking for 50 per cent of value instead, an advance of £90,000, would release £30,000 and usually earn a better rate, because the lender is less exposed.

Refinancing a balloon payment before it falls due

The commonest reason to refinance a valuable car is the final payment. Personal contract purchase and lease purchase agreements hold a large sum back to the end, and an owner who wants to keep the car has three choices: write one big cheque, hand the car back, or refinance that sum.

Take a hypothetical final payment of £38,000 on a car now valued at £110,000. The balance is about 35 per cent of value, comfortably inside the band a lender works to, so the security is not the obstacle. Spread over 48 months at 8.9 per cent, it costs about £944 a month and £45,304 in total. Over 36 months the payment rises to about £1,207 and the total falls to £43,438.

Timing is where people come unstuck. The valuation, the settlement figure and the paperwork take 1 to 3 weeks when everything goes smoothly. Starting two or three months before the final payment is due leaves room for an inspection to slip. Starting in the last fortnight turns a routine refinance into a rushed one, and rushed applications cost more.

Who decides whether the refinance is regulated?

Under the Consumer Credit Act 1974, a £126,000 advance can fall on either side of the regulated line, depending on why the money is borrowed rather than how much it is. An agreement entered into wholly or predominantly for business purposes sits outside regulated consumer credit, while borrowing for personal use normally sits inside it. Business refinances we arrange directly. Personal ones we introduce to an FCA authorised broker partner, which handles the regulated activity and any advice. You are told which route applies before you sign anything.

Whichever route it takes, the written terms should show the same essentials: the amount, the rate, the term, the total payable, the fee and the cost of settling early.

How the car refinance runs, from first call to the old account closing

It starts with a short call about the car, its likely value, what is owed and what the refinance should achieve. Nothing goes near your credit file at this point.

We then take the case only to lenders likely to want it. A lender happy to refinance a current model may have no appetite for a car that left production fifteen years ago, and every declined application leaves a mark on the file. Written terms usually arrive within 2 to 5 working days.

Once you accept, the lender carries out its valuation, then settles the old agreement directly with the outgoing lender, so two agreements are never live on the car at once. Its security is registered, any surplus is paid out, and the old account closes as settled. FAQ

Is it ever worth it to refinance a car? Yes, when the total payable on the new agreement, fees included, is lower than finishing the old one, or when the cash flow you gain is worth the extra cost and you have seen that cost in writing. Refinancing simply to stretch the term almost always costs more overall.

How much would a £30,000 car loan cost per month? At an indicative 8.9 per cent over 48 months, about £745 a month and £35,766 in total, before any fees. The rate on a real agreement depends on the car, the borrower and the share of value borrowed, so treat that as arithmetic rather than a quote.

Is it possible to refinance a car that still has finance on it? Yes, and that is the usual case. The new lender uses the settlement figure to clear the old agreement directly. What you need is a car worth comfortably more than that settlement figure.

How many times can you refinance a car? There is no set limit. Each refinance brings its own fees, possibly an early settlement charge on the agreement being replaced and a fresh credit search, and the car has to keep carrying enough value to secure the new balance. Refinancing the same car again and again usually means paying for the same months more than once.
